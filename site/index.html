Dreamcast audio and the Manatee sound driver

A guided tour of the Dreamcast's audio subsystem and of Manatee, the sound driver Sega shipped with its SDK and that a large part of the Dreamcast library runs on the sound processor: from SH-4 game code asking for a sound, across the G2 bus, into a hand-written ARM kernel that plays music, sound effects and streams on 64 hardware voices and a programmable reverb DSP.

One driver, many games

Games built on Sega's Katana SDK did not usually write their own sound engine: they linked Sega's sd library on the SH-4 and loaded a Manatee driver image (manatee.drv) into the AICA. Different SDK releases shipped different Manatee builds. Everything here is taken from one concrete, fully decompiled build, v1.1 build 0x3F, dated 1999-03-18, as shipped in Soul Calibur (USA). The architecture and concepts apply to Manatee in general; exact details such as specific bugs, command codes or table layouts may differ in other versions.

The big picture

The Dreamcast has two processors involved in sound. The SH-4 runs the game and Sega's sound library; it never touches a sound voice directly. The AICA is a Yamaha sound chip with its own ARM7DI CPU, 64 sample-playback voices, an effects DSP, a mixer and 2 MB of dedicated sound RAM. The SH-4 loads a driver program into sound RAM, starts the ARM, and from then on talks to it only through shared memory.

A sound's journey in one paragraph

A game calls, say, sdShotPlay(). The SH-4 library writes a 16-byte command into a queue in main RAM; once per video frame it copies the whole batch across the G2 bus into a mailbox in sound RAM and raises a flag byte. Within 4 ms the ARM driver notices the flag, decodes the command, picks a free AICA channel, copies a pre-built register image of the sample into it and sets its key-on bit. The voice starts reading sample data from sound RAM, shapes it with its envelope and filter, and sends it both straight to the mixer and into the DSP, where a reverb program adds the room. The driver writes a status record back into sound RAM so the game can see that the sound is playing.

Where this comes from

Everything about the driver was established by decompiling the Soul Calibur copy of it: the image was extracted from the game's CINIT.DAT, analysed in Ghidra, and rewritten as C in src/arm/, with every function independently re-checked against the disassembly. The SH-4 side comes from disassembling Sega's sound library as linked into the game. General AICA hardware behaviour (what a register does) is standard Dreamcast knowledge and is marked as such where it matters. The Bugs, quirks & confidence page lists what is certain and what is not.
